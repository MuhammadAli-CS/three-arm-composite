\section*{Summary}

Every problem here is $f(x)=g(h(x))$ with $h$ expensive and unknown and $g$
cheap and known. Two surrogates compete: \textbf{Direct} puts a GP on $f$;
\textbf{Composite} puts a GP on $h$ and pushes it through $g$.

Three findings, in order of how much they change the picture.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{Composite's advantage on bijective maps was an artefact of the
setup, not a property of the problem.} An earlier version of this document
reported that Composite wins almost everywhere, bijective or not. In that grid
Composite was handed the generator's true kernel while Direct had to estimate
one, and $h$ was drawn from an RBF kernel while both surrogates assumed
Mat\'ern. Remove both advantages and the bijective column collapses: five of
six bijective maps now show no effect at all. On \texttt{identity} the gain
fell from $+0.409$ to $+0.087\pm0.155$.

\item \textbf{With that fixed, bijectivity does predict the gain.} All ten
non-bijective maps gain, every one significant at $0.05$, by up to $3.4$
orders of magnitude. Five of six bijective maps do not.

\item \textbf{But information is not the mechanism.} The number of preimages
$\bar n$ has essentially no correlation with the size of the gain
($r=-0.12$). What does predict it is how \emph{flat $g$ is at its own
optimum} ($r=+0.78$). The two come apart cleanly on \texttt{shiftsq\_c3},
which is bijective in practice and still gains $+2.36$, and on \texttt{sum},
which destroys information and gains nothing.

\item \textbf{None of the magnitudes survive the move to two objectives.}
Run as one of two objectives under hypervolume, the same seventeen maps give
gains uncorrelated with their single-objective ones ($r=-0.19$,
$\rho=-0.00$), and the plateau measure stops working ($r=-0.36$). The
bijective/non-bijective split still shows, weakly. Section~\ref{sec:mo}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The rest of the document gives the setup, the problems, the numbers
and the evidence for each claim.

\section{The setup}

\[
f(x) \;=\; g\bigl(h(x)\bigr), \qquad x \in [0,1]^6 ,
\]
with $h$ the \emph{intermediate} (expensive, unknown) and $g$ the \emph{outer
map} (cheap, known in closed form).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
 & Observes & Models & Predicts $f$ by \\
\midrule
\textbf{Direct} & $f(x_i)$ only & a GP on $f$ & reading off that GP \\
\textbf{Composite} & $h(x_i)$ & a GP on each $h_j$ & sampling $h$ and applying $g$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Both get the same inputs, the same budget of $45$ evaluations, the
same initial design and the same acquisition function, and \textbf{both fit
their own kernel hyperparameters by maximum likelihood}. That last point is
what changed. Only the surrogate differs.

\subsection{The intermediates}

$h$ is not an analytic test function. It is a draw from the \emph{same}
Gaussian process family the surrogates assume, built from random Fourier
features:
\[
h(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M} a_m\cos(\omega_m^\top x + b_m),
\qquad a_m\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\ b_m\sim U(0,2\pi),
\]
with $M=1024$ and $\ell=0.5$, then standardised to mean $0$ and standard
deviation $1$.

The frequencies $\omega_m$ are drawn from the kernel's spectral density. For
RBF that is Gaussian; for Mat\'ern-$\nu$ it is a multivariate Student-$t$ with
$2\nu$ degrees of freedom, which is what makes Mat\'ern draws rougher.
\textbf{We use Mat\'ern-$5/2$, because that is BoTorch's default kernel.} With
an RBF draw the surrogates are merely close to correct about $h$; with a
Mat\'ern draw they are exactly correct, and the comparison is no longer
confounded by a mismatch that happens to hurt one arm more than the other.

Drawing $h$ from the surrogate's own prior is the point of the design. If $h$
were Rastrigin, Composite might win or lose because a GP models \emph{that
function} well or badly, and we would learn nothing about $g$. Here
Composite's model of $h$ is correct by construction, so every remaining
difference is attributable to the outer map. Standardising puts the mean at
zero so $h$ takes both signs; without that, $h^2$ would be injective over the
visited region and the question would be empty.

\paragraph{Range.} Over $[0,1]^6$, $h$ reaches roughly $[-4.3,+3.8]$, with
$5\%$ of the domain beyond $|h|>2$ and $0.3\%$ beyond $|h|>3$. The range
saturates --- sampling a million points finds no more than ten thousand ---
because it is a property of the function, not the sampling. That is why a root
at $\pm2$ sits inside the bulk while a vertex at $h=3$ sits at the edge of what
is reachable, which turns out to matter a great deal.

\paragraph{Several intermediates.} When $k>1$ each $h_j$ is an independent
draw with its own seed. Maps sharing a draw seed share the same $h_1$, so
switching $g$ never changes the underlying function.

\subsection{What is measured}

\paragraph{Regret.} Simple regret $R_T=f_{\text{best}}(T)-f^\star$ after
$T=45$ evaluations, reported as $\log_{10}R_T$; lower is better. Because it is
a log, a \emph{gain} of $+2$ means Composite's final regret is $100$ times
smaller. The reference optimum $f^\star$ is computed exactly for $k=1$: the
domain is connected and $h$ continuous, so $h([0,1]^6)$ is an interval and
$\min_x g(h(x))$ is the minimum of $g$ over that interval, which a dense grid
finds for any $g$ --- including the ones a gradient method cannot descend.

\paragraph{$\bar n$.} The average number of $h$ values that could have produced
the observed $y$,
\[
\bar n=\mathbb E\bigl[\,|g^{-1}(y)|\,\bigr],
\]
\emph{measured} by counting crossings of $g(z)-y$ over the range $h$ actually
reaches, not inferred from the algebra. The two come apart:
\texttt{shiftsq\_c3} is a parabola, two-to-one on paper, but its vertex sits at
$h=3$ where $h$ almost never goes, so in practice $\bar n=1.01$.

\paragraph{The $p$-value.} A one-sided paired $t$-test on the per-run
difference, asking whether Composite's mean regret is lower. Paired because
both arms share an initial design within each run, so the large variation
between designs cancels. $20$ runs per problem: $4$ independent function draws
$\times$ $5$ initial designs.

\paragraph{The spread.} Quoted as $\pm$ one standard deviation across the $20$
runs, not a standard error --- it describes how much a single run varies. The
standard error is roughly $1/\sqrt{20}$ of it.

\subsection{What is excluded, and why}

Four maps are kept out of the headline comparison.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \texttt{relu}, \texttt{abs} and \texttt{clip\_c2} are not everywhere

\item \texttt{cos\_w4} is deferred for separate investigation.

\noindent This must be stated plainly: \textbf{the three non-smooth maps are

non-bijective group from ten significant rows out of thirteen to ten out of
ten. The criterion is defensible on its own terms, decided by smoothness of
$g$ rather than by the result, but it does flatter the grouping, so both

\subsection{The linear control}

\texttt{sum}, $g(h_1,h_2)=h_1+h_2$, is the decisive row. Observing
$y=h_1+h_2$ tells you the sum but not the split, so information genuinely
\emph{is} destroyed --- and yet the gain is $-0.230$, in Direct's favour.

The reason is that a linear combination of Gaussian processes is a Gaussian
process. Direct's model is therefore correctly specified, and it loses
nothing by not seeing the pieces. \textbf{Information loss with no
misspecification buys Composite nothing.} Whatever drives the gain elsewhere,
it is not the mere fact that $g$ is many-to-one.

\section{Why bijectivity predicts the gain without being the cause}
\label{sec:plateau}

Two observations do not fit an information story.

\paragraph{$\bar n$ does not order the gain.} Across the single-intermediate
maps the correlation between $\bar n$ and the gain is $r=-0.12$ --- no
relationship at all. \texttt{cos\_w2} has $\bar n=5.04$ and gains $+1.06$;
\texttt{root2} has $\bar n=2.00$ and gains $+3.42$.

\paragraph{\texttt{shiftsq\_c3} gains without being many-to-one.} It measures
$\bar n=1.01$, so a typical observation determines $h$, and it still gains
$+2.36$ --- the largest gain of any map outside the \texttt{root}/\texttt{shiftsq}
families.

\subsection{What does predict it}

The quantity that works is how flat $g$ is \emph{at its own minimum}. Measure
the plateau width: the fraction of $h$'s reachable range mapping to within
$10^{-6}$ of $\min g$.

\input{plateau_table.tex}

\noindent The table separates into two blocks with nothing in between. Above
the rule every map has a genuine plateau and the mean gain is $+2.05$; below
it the width is at the grid resolution, meaning the minimum is a single point,
and the mean gain is $+0.10$. The correlation is $r=+0.78$, against $r=-0.12$
for $\bar n$.

\subsection{The mechanism}

Composite models $h$ accurately. Near a flat optimum a small error in $h$
produces a very small error in $f$, so an accurate model of $h$ converts into
enormous depth in objective space. Direct, looking at $f$ alone, sees a nearly
constant region and cannot resolve within it. Where $g$ is steep at its
optimum there is no such leverage and both arms do the same thing.

This explains why bijectivity predicts the gain anyway. A bijective $g$ on an
interval is monotone; a monotone function attains its minimum at an endpoint
of the range; and an endpoint is generically a steep place, not a flat one. So

\begin{center}
bijective $\Rightarrow$ monotone $\Rightarrow$ optimum at an endpoint
$\Rightarrow$ steep there $\Rightarrow$ no plateau $\Rightarrow$ no gain.
\end{center}

\noindent Bijectivity forecloses a flat optimum. That is why the prediction
holds, and it is not an information argument at any step.

\texttt{shiftsq\_c3} is the case that distinguishes the two explanations. Its
fold sits at $h=3$, at the very edge of where $h$ reaches, so almost every
observation lands on the monotone left branch and $\bar n\approx1$. But its
\emph{minimum} is exactly at that fold, where $g$ is flat. $\bar n$ averages
over the whole distribution of $y$, which is dominated by regions far from the
optimum, so it misses the one place that matters. The plateau measure looks
only at the optimum, and gets it right.

\section{Regression error and optimisation gain come apart}

The clearest evidence that this is not about model quality on average: fit
both models by plain regression, with no optimiser anywhere, and compare the
held-out error with the BO gain on the same problem.

\input{rmse_table.tex}

Three things to read off it.

\paragraph{The inner RMSE is $0.786$ in every row.} It must be: $h$ is
literally the same function in all of them, and Composite always fits the same
GP to it. A check that nothing else is varying.

\paragraph{\texttt{mono3} shows real misspecification and no gain.} Direct
scores $1.110$ --- worse than predicting the mean of $f$ --- while Composite
scores $0.976$ on the same eighty points with its own fitted hyperparameters.
Nothing is hidden from Direct: $h^3$ is bijective, so $y$ determines $h$
exactly. The gap is purely that $f=g(h)$ is \emph{not a Gaussian process} when
$g$ is nonlinear, so Direct is fitting the wrong shape of model. And the BO
gain is $+0.081$, which is nothing. \textbf{Misspecification is real here and
does not convert into optimisation performance.}

\paragraph{\texttt{shiftsq\_c3} shows no misspecification and a huge gain.}
$0.794$ against $0.782$ --- the two models are equally good at predicting $f$
on average --- and the BO gain is $+2.36$. \texttt{mono5} makes the same point
from the other side: the largest regression gap in the table, $1.996$ against
$1.570$, and a BO gain of $-0.048$.

\medskip
\noindent So average predictive accuracy and optimisation gain are decoupled.
What matters is resolution \emph{at the optimum}, which is what the plateau
measures and what neither $\bar n$ nor RMSE captures.
\texttt{identity} is the control throughout: $0.786$ against $0.786$,
identical to three figures, because there $f=h$ and the two models are the
same model.

\section{The two-objective setting}
\label{sec:mo}

Everything so far minimises a single objective. The same maps were then run
as the first of two objectives,
\[
f_1(x)=g_1\bigl(h_1(x)\bigr), \qquad f_2(x)=h_2(x),
\]
with $h_1,h_2$ independent draws from the same prior and performance measured
by hypervolume against a reference point shared by both arms. The second
objective is deliberately the identity, so both arms model it with the same GP
and every difference between them has to come from $g_1$ --- the same
isolation the single-objective design uses.

Twenty-one maps, $4$ draws $\times$ $5$ designs. The map set is matched to the
single-objective grid on purpose: of the $21$, seventeen are the same
functions measured earlier, verified identical to machine precision rather
than re-implemented by eye.

\subsection{The two settings do not agree}

\input{matched_table.tex}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{../figures/so_vs_mo.pdf}
\caption{The same seventeen maps in both settings. If the single-objective
result predicted the multi-objective one, the points would trend upward. They

\noindent Across the seventeen shared maps the two gains are
\textbf{uncorrelated}: Pearson $r=-0.19$, Spearman $\rho=-0.00$; dropping
\texttt{root5}, $-0.05$ and $+0.02$. \texttt{root5} gains $+0.42$ in regret
and $+31\%$ in hypervolume; \texttt{shiftsq\_c2} gains $+2.78$ in regret and
$+0.17\%$ in hypervolume. Knowing how a map behaves in one setting tells you
almost nothing about the other.

\paragraph{The plateau mechanism does not transfer, as predicted.} Plateau
width explains the single-objective gain at $r=+0.78$ and the hypervolume gain
at $r=-0.36$ ($-0.21$ without \texttt{root5}). This is the expected direction:
the argument of Section~\ref{sec:plateau} is about the neighbourhood of a
single optimum, and hypervolume has no single optimum --- the Pareto front
spans the whole attained range of $g_1$. \texttt{shiftsq\_c3} confirms it from
the inside: first of seventeen by single-objective gain, eighth of twenty-one
by hypervolume gain.

\paragraph{\texttt{root5} is a scale artefact, not structure.} Its $+31\%$ is
by far the largest effect in the grid, and it comes from the objective being
extremely heavy-tailed even after percentile normalisation: the ratio of its
extreme value to its $99$th percentile is $256$, against $2$--$10$ for a
typical map. One lucky extreme evaluation moves the hypervolume a long way.
That single point is also a trap for the analysis --- tail-heaviness appears
to explain the gains at $r=+0.94$, which collapses to $-0.02$ once
\texttt{root5} is removed, with Spearman only $+0.15$. It explains
\texttt{root5} and nothing else.

\paragraph{What does survive.} The grouping, weakly.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
 & Single objective & Multi objective \\
\midrule
bijective ($n=6$) & $+0.45$, \textbf{0 of 6} significant & $+1.19\%$, \textbf{0 of 6} significant \\
not bijective ($n=11$) & $+1.80$, \textbf{10 of 11} & $+4.28\%$, \textbf{5 of 11} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Same direction in both settings, much weaker under hypervolume. So
the proposition's qualitative prediction carries over; the quantitative
structure behind it does not. Beyond \texttt{root5}, nothing tested here ---
$\bar n$, plateau width, tail-heaviness, or the single-objective gain itself
--- predicts the size of the hypervolume gain.

\section{What this means}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{The proposition's prediction holds.} Bijective $g$: no gain, in
five of six cases. Non-bijective $g$: a gain every time, up to $3.4$ orders of
magnitude. The same split appears in the two-objective setting, weaker.
\item \textbf{Its stated reason does not.} $\bar n$ is uncorrelated with the
size of the gain, \texttt{sum} destroys information and gains nothing, and
\texttt{shiftsq\_c3} preserves it and gains a great deal.
\item \textbf{The operative property is flatness of $g$ at its optimum.}
Bijectivity predicts the gain because it rules that out, not because of
anything to do with information. This is specific to the single-objective
setting: the same measure is uninformative about hypervolume
(Section~\ref{sec:mo}).
\item \textbf{The earlier three-channel decomposition is withdrawn.} The
split into hyperparameter estimation, model shape and information was measured
on a grid where Composite held the true kernel; with both arms fitting, there
is no bijective-case gain left to decompose.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Still open.} \texttt{cos\_w4} has the most preimages of any map
($\bar n=9.75$) and a plateau, yet gains nothing ($-0.014$). The likely reason
is that at $\omega=4$ the posterior uncertainty in $h$ spans most of a
half-period, so Composite's predictive on $f$ becomes as wide as $f$ itself
and knowing $h$ stops telling it anything about the objective. That is a
budget question --- $\mathrm{sd}(h\mid\text{data})$ shrinks as data arrives
--- and is being investigated separately.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All numbers come from
\texttt{three\_arm/results\_matern2} ($22$ maps $\times$ $4$ draws $\times$ $5$
seeds). Every table in this document is generated by
\texttt{python three\_arm/tables.py}, so it cannot drift from the stored
results. The grid was verified before use: $f^\star$ recomputes to $10^{-9}$
on all $88$ conditions, $h$ is shared across maps at each draw, no run has
negative regret, and none sits at the regret floor.
